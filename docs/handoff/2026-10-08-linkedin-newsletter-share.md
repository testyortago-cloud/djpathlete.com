## Before (what production does today)

### "Knowledge Is Not Power" (221 words; the issue is 358)

Authority and understanding are not the same thing. That distinction matters more in coaching than most of us admit.

Foucault's argument was that power shapes what gets accepted as knowledge — not just the other way around. Ideas repeated by respected institutions become "the way things are" through familiarity and reinforcement, not necessarily through rigorous evidence. Strength training makes athletes slow. Bed rest fixes low back pain. Static stretching before activity prevents injury. Each was taught, defended, and widely accepted by credentialed professionals. Each has since been substantially revised. The mechanism isn't incompetence. It's the slow replacement of curiosity with certainty.

Most coaches will tell you experience is the best teacher. Here's where I'd push back: experience is only as good as your willingness to examine it. A coach who's run the same drill for 20 years has 20 years of reinforced habit — not necessarily 20 years of evidence. Socrates wasn't being self-deprecating when he said he knew nothing. He was describing the operating posture that keeps you correctable. The strongest frameworks aren't the ones that avoid scrutiny. They're the ones that survive it.

The question worth sitting with: where in your coaching are you following a belief because of who said it, rather than because you've genuinely examined it yourself?

### "Performance is a Transitional State" (232 words; the issue is 285)

Most performance breakdowns aren't performance problems. They're transition problems.

That distinction matters more than most coaches give it credit for. A body that tolerated 80% intensity last block doesn't anymore. An athlete moves up a competitive level and the margin for error compresses overnight. An injury changes trust in the tissue, not just the tissue itself. The context shifts, but the program stays the same. That's where things go wrong.

The reflex in this industry is to respond with more: more volume, more intensity, more urgency. That's exactly the wrong move in a transition phase. Specificity doesn't mean pressing harder. It means matching the stimulus to the actual state of the athlete in front of you right now, not the one from 12 weeks ago.

Most coaches will tell you the best programs are built around peak outputs and optimal progressions. Here's where I disagree: a program designed for a stable training environment will produce friction, not adaptation, when the athlete is mid-transition. Growth spurts, return-to-sport timelines, new competition levels, aging, reduced recovery windows. These aren't obstacles to the program. They ARE the program.

The better question isn't "what's the best training plan?" It's "how am I reading this athlete right now, and what decision makes sense given that?"
